Alexander Theodore Shulgin

Sasha Shulgin

An industrial pesticide chemist who spent fifty years synthesising psychoactive compounds in a shed behind his house and testing them on himself. The psychedelic-therapy revival claims him and the research-chemicals market can be traced to him, and both are fair. He did not invent MDMA. He worked out what it did and told psychotherapists about it.

In brief

Four things worth carrying away, if you read nothing else.

  • He made his own freedom first. The carbamate insecticide Zectran, invented at Dow and marketed in 1961, was profitable enough that the company told him to work on whatever he liked. He worked on psychedelics.
  • Roughly two hundred compounds, most of them tested on himself. The 2C family and the DOx family are his; so is the first published account of what MDMA does in a human being.
  • The books are the whole controversy in one object. PiHKAL and TiHKAL are half memoir, half laboratory record, with synthesis and dosage printed in full. The DEA read them as manuals. So, later, did a global market in novel psychoactive substances.
  • His reputation is being actively rewritten. MDMA-assisted therapy reached formal regulatory review, his archive is being digitised, and historians have both credited him and punctured his self-mythology.

Life before the Farm

Berkeley, Harvard at sixteen, the North Atlantic, and eleven years of pesticide chemistry.

  • Born 17 June 1925 in Berkeley. His father, Theodore Stevens Shulgin, was a Russian émigré from Chelyabinsk who taught history and literature; his mother, Henrietta Aten, was an Illinois-born poet. Both taught in Alameda County public schools.
  • Music and a chemistry set. He played piano, violin and viola throughout his life. A Gilbert Chemical Laboratory Outfit became his first laboratory, in his parents' basement.
  • Harvard at sixteen, on scholarship. He read organic chemistry from about 1942, felt out of place among the children of wealth and position, and left in his second year, in 1943.
  • The Navy, 1943–46. He served in the North Atlantic aboard the USS Pope.
  • Berkeley, then UCSF. A B.S. in chemistry and a PhD in biochemistry completed in 1954 — the degree is often dated 1955 — under David M. Greenberg, with a thesis on the synthesis of isotopically labelled amino acids, followed by postdoctoral work in psychiatry and pharmacology at UC San Francisco.
  • Dow Chemical, roughly 1955 to 1966. He joined as a senior research chemist on pesticides and fertilisers, after a short spell as research director at Bio-Rad Laboratories.
  • Zectran. His carbamate insecticide mexacarbate, marketed by Dow in 1961 and described as the first biodegradable pesticide, was highly profitable. Of the latitude the patent bought him he told The Independent in 2002 that Dow said "Do as you wish. So I did. I did psychedelics."
  • 2 April 1960: mescaline. His first psychedelic experience was decisive. He described understanding that "our entire universe is contained in the mind and the spirit" — and that chemicals could open access to it. He said afterwards that it settled the direction of his whole life.
  • The exit, late 1966. As the psychedelic papers multiplied, Dow asked him to stop attaching the company's name to them, and he left. Months later DOM, a compound of his, surfaced in Haight-Ashbury as "STP" and was traced back to him.

The origin story he told most often was about a placebo. Before surgery on an infected thumb in the Navy, a nurse gave him orange juice; he took the undissolved sugar at the bottom of the glass for a sedative and passed out. Working out what had actually happened left him permanently interested in the machinery of mental process rather than in chemistry as such.

The Farm and the DEA

A shed behind the house, a Schedule I licence, and a working relationship with the agency that eventually took it away.

  • The Farm. A small outbuilding behind his house on family land near Lafayette, California. He spent about two years studying neurology at the UCSF School of Medicine before going full-time as a private consultant.
  • A Schedule I analytical licence. It let him legally synthesise and hold otherwise-illicit compounds. Everything that followed rested on it.
  • Teaching paid for the rest. Public health, forensic toxicology, chemistry and the pharmacology of psychoactive drugs at UC Berkeley, UCSF, San Francisco State and San Francisco General; briefly a pharmaceutical chemistry post at San Jose State in 1971. Ann later transcribed his SFSU lectures, published after his death as The Nature of Drugs.

Deputised

Through Bob Sager, head of the DEA's Western Laboratories, he ran pharmacology seminars for agents, supplied reference samples and appeared as an expert witness in drug trials.

He collected DEA commendations, including one in 1973 for personal efforts against drug abuse, and in 1988 wrote Controlled Substances, then the standard chemical-and-legal reference for federal drug law. A DEA official married him and Ann in 1981.

Raided

On 27 October 1994, around thirty federal, state and local agents arrived unannounced with warrants — eight vehicles, including a fire engine and marked police cars — a month after a routine inspection.

Framed as an administrative investigation of regulatory compliance rather than a criminal one, it produced record-keeping and storage infractions. He surrendered the licence rather than fight a costly and unwinnable case, and was fined $25,000 by the local district attorney.

  • The fine was for other people's mail. Strangers posted him anonymous samples hoping he would test them. An unpublished DEA administrative rule barred holding them, at up to $25,000 per sample.
  • His community paid it. The fine plus roughly $15,000 in legal and related costs came out of the couple's retirement savings; supporters set up a trust and raised at least $28,000.
  • Two earlier inspections of the same laboratory had found nothing. Both predated PiHKAL, which the DEA read as a synthesis manual — the main reason most accounts treat the raid as a response to the book rather than to the shed.

The work

Roughly two hundred compounds, a five-point scale for describing them, and a method most of his field would not touch.

2C
phenethylamines

His own name for the family, after the two carbons between the benzene ring and the amino group. 2C-B (1974) is the best known; 2C-E, 2C-I, 2C-T-2 and 2C-T-7 followed.

Asked in his last interview to name a single favourite compound, he said 2C-B without hesitating.

DOx
phenethylamines

Begun with DOM, first synthesised in 1963. In mid-1967 it reached Haight-Ashbury as "STP", given away free by the underground chemist Owsley Stanley at strengths far above anything Shulgin had used, and produced a wave of bad reactions and hospitalisations.

The first case of a Shulgin compound escaping into uncontrolled use, and a template for what happened later. A peer-reviewed account published in 2024 revisited the episode and his part in it.

Tryptamines
the second book

Seminal work on the tryptamine series, gathered in TiHKAL in 1997. The published derivatives seeded a second wave of novel substances in the same way the phenethylamines had.

  • The Shulgin Rating Scale. A qualitative ladder — minus, plus-or-minus, plus, plus-plus, plus-plus-plus — for reporting subjective intensity, published with Ann Shulgin and Peyton Jacob III in 1986 in Methods and Findings in Experimental and Clinical Pharmacology and popularised by PiHKAL.
  • A separate mark for something else entirely. Four pluses denotes a rare transcendental peak experience rather than a stronger effect. Across an estimated four thousand sessions over fifty years, he claimed two or three.
  • The magical half-dozen. Mescaline, DOM, 2C-B, 2C-E, 2C-T-2 and 2C-T-7 — the compounds he returned to.
  • The method. A new compound went to him first, beginning far below anything he expected to be active and escalating slowly, then to a small circle of trusted friends including Ann and Myron and Jean Stolaroff. Everyone wrote up what happened. Those write-ups were the data.

The defence

Self-experimentation is an old and respectable scientific tradition — Purkyně and others — and the neuroscientist Matthew Baggott and others have argued that Shulgin was stigmatised chiefly for being open about doing what others did quietly.

Nobody else was going to characterise these compounds. Without him, most would simply be unknown.

The charge

Chemistry World called the practice "rather troubling": doses reasoned from structural resemblance to a known analogue, with no pharmacokinetic data from animal models behind them.

Drake Bennett, profiling him in the New York Times Magazine in 2005, reported that most of the scientific community regarded him as "at best a curiosity and at worst a menace."

MDMA

The claim made about him most often is the one most often got wrong. The order of events settles it.

MDMA was first synthesised at Merck as a synthetic intermediate. Its effects were left essentially unexplored for the next half-century.

He synthesised it himself but did not test it. It sat untouched for eleven years.

A student in the medicinal-chemistry group he advised at San Francisco State mentioned a "special effect". He resynthesised the compound and took it himself in September 1976; his laboratory notebook confirms the date.

The Oakland psychologist came out of retirement for it and called it "Adam", believing it returned people to a state of primordial innocence.

Over the next twelve years, until MDMA was prohibited in 1985, Zeff treated more than four thousand patients with it and trained roughly a hundred and fifty therapists — the figure cited by both the UC Berkeley Center for the Science of Psychedelics and the American Journal of Psychiatry.

The first published account of MDMA's pharmacological action in humans. This, not the synthesis, is the contribution that stands.

MDMA was scheduled. Rick Doblin recalls Shulgin being badly depressed by it: his view was that these compounds opened people not to drug experiences but, in his phrasing, "to human experiences of ourselves."

Benzenhöfer and Passie, writing in Addiction, put it plainly: "Shulgin was not the first to synthesize MDMA". He played an important part in its history, which is why he is sometimes wrongly called its father.

If you only ever say one thing about Shulgin and MDMA, say reintroduced — not invented, and not father of.

The books

Two volumes, each half novel and half laboratory record, published by the Shulgins themselves because nobody else would touch them.

PiHKAL
1991 · phenethylamines

"Phenethylamines I Have Known and Loved." Part one is a fictionalised autobiography of Shura and Alice, thinly veiled versions of the Shulgins, love triangle included; the main fiction, as he put it, is that it is fiction.

Part two is technical: 179 compounds with synthesis, suggested dosage, duration and detailed commentary on effects.

TiHKAL
1997 · tryptamines

"Tryptamines I Have Known and Loved", in the same two-part shape: 55 tryptamine entries plus appendices on cactus alkaloids, natural beta-carbolines and drug law.

The technical half was made freely available on Erowid; the memoir stayed in print only.

Controlled Substances
1988 · reference

A chemical and legal guide to federal drug law, and for a time the standard desk reference for narcotics officers.

The Simple Plant Isoquinolines
2002 · with Wendy Perry

624 pages, cross-indexed, on plant isoquinoline alkaloids including the cactus mescaline analogues. Written with his stepdaughter.

The Shulgin Index
2011 · with Manning and Daley

Volume one, roughly a thousand pages on psychedelic phenethylamines and related compounds. A reference work rather than a memoir.

The Nature of Drugs
posthumous · lectures

His San Francisco State lectures, transcribed by Ann and published by Transform Press. He also contributed a chapter, "A New Vocabulary", to Entheogens and the Future of Religion in 1997.

  • Why they self-published. Journals had grown wary of his work on legal grounds and publishers thought PiHKAL too dangerous, so the Shulgins founded Transform Press in 1991 to put it out themselves.
  • Why he published at all. He cited Wilhelm Reich, dead in prison in 1957, as the cautionary case: better to put the knowledge in the open than let it be confiscated.

The foreword

David Nichols wrote that there was no other book like it — and that new legislation meant there might never be another.

Both volumes became foundational texts, read as readily in academic laboratories as in the underground.

The verdict

Richard Meyer, spokesman for the DEA's San Francisco field division, said the books were "pretty much cookbooks on how to make illegal drugs".

He added that agents kept finding copies in the clandestine laboratories they raided.

Ann, and the Tribe

The work was not done alone, and the second author was not a footnote.

  • His first wife, Nina Gordon, died in 1977. Their son Theodore died in 2011.
  • Ann. Born Laura Ann Gotlieb in Wellington in 1931, to an American consul, Bernard Gotlieb, and a New Zealander mother; raised in Italy, Cuba, Canada and the United States. She worked as an artist and a medical transcriber, and later as a lay therapist facilitating MDMA and 2C-B sessions while both were still legal.
  • They met in 1978 and married on 4 July 1981, in the back garden at the Farm. It was her fourth marriage; she had four children.
  • The division of labour was explicit. In her words: "He has the scientific viewpoint, and I have the psychological and the spiritual." She co-wrote both books and co-founded Transform Press.
  • The Tribe. A standing group of therapists, scientists and friends who sat for sessions and wrote them up, some of them for decades.
  • The end. He died of liver cancer at the Farm on 2 June 2014, aged 88, with family, carers and Buddhist meditation music in the room. Ann died in the same house on 9 July 2022, aged 91, after a long decline with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Parts of the property had been sold to cover his late medical costs, the rest made up by donations from their community.

Legacy

Both of these are true simultaneously, and an honest account has to hold them together rather than pick one.

What he opened

MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD was built on the ground he and Nichols cleared. Rick Doblin petitioned the DEA over MDMA's scheduling in 1984 and founded MAPS; the work later earned FDA breakthrough therapy designation and reached phase 3 trials, with MAPS reporting around 1,700 subjects dosed and a single serious adverse reaction.

Timothy Leary told the Los Angeles Times in 1995 that he considered the Shulgins "two of the most important scientists of the 20th Century".

What he loosed

Historians date the "phenethylamine period" of illicit derivatives — roughly fifty of them turning up in European and American seizures — to PiHKAL's publication, with tryptamine derivatives following TiHKAL. Later and far more potent derivatives, notably the NBOMe class active at microgram scale, were linked to deaths.

Compounds he first described, particularly 2C-T-7 and 2C-I, were implicated in overdose deaths. He expressed sadness but argued that every drug, aspirin included, is dangerous used wrongly. Mark Peplow, in Chemistry World, judged those later disclaimers naïve at best.

  • The chemistry establishment's verdict is mixed and unembarrassed about it. Obituaries in C&EN and Chemistry World credit a highly skilled synthetic chemist and a pioneer of structure–activity work on 5-HT2A-active phenethylamines, while being blunt about the method. Nichols's continuing medicinal chemistry rests on the same groundwork.
  • Dirty Pictures (2010). Étienne Sauret's documentary, premiered at SXSW, featuring the Shulgins, David Nichols, William Fantegrossi, John Mendelson and the former DEA agent Bob Sager. The title is Shulgin's own term for his chemical structure drawings.
  • The Shulgin Archive. Notebooks, personal papers, photographs and media are being digitised by the Erowid Center — Earth and Fire Erowid, with Keeper Trout leading the scanning. Over $340,000 spent since 2015; the bulk of the scanning was finished by the end of 2024, with something over two hundred thousand documents captured and cataloguing continuing. The pharmacology notebooks, numbered 1–9 and A–F and spanning roughly 1960 to 1994, are published online.
  • Berkeley. A 2025 grant from the Center for the Science of Psychedelics, funded through the Flourish Trust, supports an MDMA exhibition at the Berkeley Alembic in 2026 — described as a first step towards folding the archive into the university's library system.

Caveats

How to use the above without repeating the errors that circulate about him.

  • Say reintroduced, not invented. Merck's 1912 priority and the 2010 reassessment in Addiction make the stronger claims simply wrong.
  • Treat his human data as uncontrolled self-report, often with a sample of one, unblinded — not as clinical evidence. The peer-reviewed Shulgin–Nichols work and the academic histories carry more weight than the admiring secondary accounts.
  • Hold both halves of the legacy. Open publication advanced legitimate research and seeded an uncontrolled global market. Any invocation of his name should carry the documented harms alongside the therapeutic promise.
  • Go to primary sources where you can. The Erowid archive and laboratory notebooks, the Transform Press editions, the C&EN and Chemistry World obituaries, the 2005 New York Times Magazine profile and the peer-reviewed histories. Treat vendor pages, forums and AI-generated reference sites as low confidence.

Sourcing

What is contested

Six specific points where sources disagree, where a common claim is out of date, or where the record leans on Shulgin's own semi-fictionalised telling. Worth opening before citing anything above.

  • The compound count varies by source. Nearly 200, over 200, over 230, nearly 250 — the spread reflects different definitions (synthesised, bioassayed, first described) and a self-reported tally.
  • The raid is sometimes dated 1993, including by PBS/Nova. Contemporaneous accounts put it at 27 October 1994.
  • MDMA-assisted therapy is not approved. In August 2024 the FDA issued a complete response letter declining the Lykos Therapeutics application for PTSD. Descriptions of a vindicated psychedelic renaissance describe a trajectory, not a finished fact.
  • Much of the biographical colour is his own. PiHKAL and TiHKAL are semi-fictionalised, and MAPS, Erowid and Transform Press are sympathetic parties. For contested points — his exact role in MDMA's history, the DOM/STP episode — prefer independent scholarship.
  • The more sensational claims are weakly sourced. That he personally supplied the DOM that reached the street is addressed in a peer-reviewed 2024 account; suggestions of DEA political protection rest mostly on secondary or partisan sources.
  • The Wikipedia article carries a maintenance flag from September 2024, for reliance on non-independent sources and a non-neutral, overly positive tone outside the lead. Corroborate anything taken from it.
