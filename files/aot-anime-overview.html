Before you start

Attack on Titan

A dark fantasy war story that begins as a siege and does not stay one. It is a finished work with a real ending, adapted closely from a manga that ran for twelve years. It is also relentlessly grim, and it is not trying to be anything else.

The premise

Everything below is established in the first two episodes. None of it is a spoiler.

  • Humanity lives inside three concentric walls. They are roughly fifty metres tall and they have held for a hundred years. Wall Maria is the outermost, then Wall Rose, then Wall Sina at the centre. Nobody living remembers the outside.
  • Outside the walls are Titans. They are naked, humanoid, and between three and fifteen metres tall. They have no visible genitals and no apparent digestive purpose. They eat people anyway.
  • They cannot be killed conventionally. Limbs regrow. Wounds close. The one weak point is a specific patch at the nape of the neck, roughly one metre by ten centimetres. Cut it deeply enough and the Titan dies.
  • The nape is at the back of the neck, high up. That single anatomical fact dictates the entire military doctrine, and it is why the show looks the way it does.
  • Soldiers fly. ODM gear is a harness with grappling hooks and pressurised gas canisters. It lets a person swing between buildings and trees, land on a Titan's shoulders, and cut down. The animation of this is the show's signature.
  • The story opens in the year 845. A Titan far larger than any recorded appears above the wall and kicks a hole in it. That is episode one.

The first season follows three childhood friends who survive that day and enlist. That is the whole setup. The show adds to it steadily and does not explain everything for a long time.

What it's like to watch

This is the part most summaries skip. The register is unusual and it is worth knowing in advance.

What it does well

The action is spatial and legible. The ODM sequences are choreographed in three dimensions and hold up.

The score by Hiroyuki Sawano is loud, choral and unsubtle, and it is a large part of why the show lands.

The plotting is tight. There is almost no filler across all 89 episodes, because it adapts a manga that was already written.

It commits. Setups pay off, sometimes forty episodes later.

What it asks of you

Named characters die constantly and without ceremony. There is no plot armour and the show establishes this early.

Children are eaten on screen. So are parents, in front of children.

There is sustained body horror, on-screen dismemberment, and in later seasons atrocity and war-crime imagery.

The early seasons are shouty and melodramatic. Some people bounce off the first few episodes for this reason.

The show is rated 17+ and earns it. It is not gore for its own sake, but it does not flinch and it does not let up.

The people

Names only, with no roles given away. You meet almost all of them in the first season.

Eren Yeager
the protagonist

Angry, stubborn, fifteen at the start. Wants to see the ocean and to kill every Titan, in roughly that order.

Mikasa Ackerman
his adoptive sister

Graduated top of her cadet class by a wide margin. Follows Eren by choice.

Armin Arlert
their friend

Physically the weakest of the three, and the one who solves things.

Levi Ackerman
captain, Survey Corps

Introduced partway through season one. Short, blunt, and the most effective soldier alive. The show's most popular character by a distance.

Erwin Smith
commander, Survey Corps

Sends people to die on purpose and knows exactly what that costs.

Hange Zoë
squad leader

Studies Titans with unsettling enthusiasm.

The 104th Cadets
the class

Jean, Sasha, Connie, Reiner, Bertholdt, Annie, Ymir, Krista, Marco. They train together in season one and the show spends real time on all of them.

The vocabulary

Terms that arrive without explanation in the first few episodes.

ODM gear
Omni-Directional Mobility gear. Older subtitles call it 3D Manoeuvre Gear or Vertical Manoeuvring Equipment. Same thing.
Survey Corps
Also translated Scout Regiment. They go outside the walls. Very high casualty rate, very low recruitment.
Garrison
Man the walls and the cannons. The largest branch.
Military Police
Stationed in the interior, away from danger. Only the top ten cadets in a class may join.
Shiganshina
The district in Wall Maria where the story starts.
Trost
A district of Wall Rose, and the setting of the board game.
Colossal Titan
The sixty-metre one from episode one. Steams constantly.
Armoured Titan
The one with plated skin. Appears alongside it.

  • The season names are marketing, not structure. "The Final Season" spans three years and four separate broadcasts. In story terms it is one continuous stretch.
  • Episodes 88 and 89 are feature-length. Roughly 60 and 85 minutes.
  • THE LAST ATTACK is a theatrical recut of those same two specials into one 145-minute film, with reworked sound and visuals and a non-canon joke scene after the credits. It covers identical story events.
  • The studio changes at episode 60. WIT hands off to MAPPA. The art style, colour grading and animation approach all shift noticeably at that point.
  • It is finished. The manga ended in April 2021 at 139 chapters. The anime adapted that ending. Nothing is left dangling and no continuation is pending.

Side material

None of it is load-bearing. The main plot never assumes you have seen any of it.

Bundled with Japanese manga volumes. Mostly side stories and character bits. "Ilse's Notebook" is the one Isayama wrote himself and the only one that touches main-plot lore.

The shape of the story

A structural map, hidden by default.

Mild structural spoilers

How it is built

No plot events, no reveals, no character fates. What follows describes how the story is shaped and where its tone changes — the kind of thing you would notice on a rewatch. Some people want this before committing 35 hours. Some would rather not know.

  • It moves in three registers. It opens as siege horror. It becomes a mystery about the walls themselves. It ends as something closer to political tragedy. The genre shift is deliberate and it is the reason the show is discussed the way it is.
  • The first shift lands early. Around a third of the way through season one, the premise you were sold changes materially. If the opening episodes feel like a straightforward monster show, that is temporary.
  • The Final Season opens cold. New setting, new characters, new time frame, and no immediate explanation. This lasts for several episodes and it is intentional, not a mistake. A lot of viewers stall here.
  • The moral framing inverts. The show gradually stops having a side you are meant to be on. Who counts as the aggressor is the central question of the back half.
  • The ending is divisive. It is one of the most argued-about endings in the medium. Both the manga and anime versions draw the same criticism. Going in aware of that is different from going in spoiled.
  • The opening credits change meaning. The season one opening in particular reads very differently once you finish. This is widely noted and is not an accident.

Where the board game sits

The Last Stand adapts one specific event from early in the series. Everything in the box comes from the first three seasons.

The scenario

The Titan breach at Trost, roughly episodes 5 to 13. The supply depot behind that wall is what makes the district worth defending.

The tower and cannons

Wall-mounted artillery is the Garrison's actual doctrine in the show. It is largely ineffective against Titans, which the game reflects: the cannons deplete and never come back.

The nine levels

A direct translation of ODM gear. Position and vertical movement are the entire tactical language of the show's combat.

Neck Slash

The nape. It is the only way to kill a Titan in the fiction, so it is the only way to win in the game.

Citizen tokens

Civilian evacuation is a live problem during the Trost arc and a recurring source of casualties.

The eight Heroes

All introduced by the end of season three. Playing the game will not spoil anything past that point.
